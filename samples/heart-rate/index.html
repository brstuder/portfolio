    Changes in American Robin Heart Rate in Response to Brood Parasite Eggs¶

    Author's Note

    This sample is an excerpt of a research paper I helped draft as a biology intern at the University of Illinois. The paper seeks to explain whether the presence of parasitic brood eggs in the nest of a host species would produce a measurable indicator of stress, in our case, by detecting any increased heart rate. After several years, the research and investigation I helped perform would constitute a revised, published version which can be read on the National Library of Medicine website.

    Abstract:¶

    Brood parasitic bird species lay their eggs in the nest of other host species and subsequently abandon them to the care of the host parents. American robins (Turdus migratorius) are parasitized by the brood-parasitic brown-headed cowbirds (Molothrus ater), but, contrary to many other species, robins almost always are able to reject cowbird eggs instead of incubating them. What we wanted to know is if the presence of cowbird eggs causes any type of stress in mother robins. We hoped to determine if cowbird parasitism induced a stress-response in robin females by measuring and recording the heart rate of incubating robins. By using 3D-printed eggs embedded with microphones, we created mimetic robin eggs which are designed to pick up the mother’s heart rate without distressing her. This was done by flushing the mother from her nest and then replacing one of her eggs with a microphone-embedded egg. After one day of habituation, we returned with an additional model egg, either a cyan egg or a beige egg, designed to imitate either robin or cowbird eggs respectively. The model egg sits for two hours, and then we return to swap the egg out with the opposite color. We recorded the audio from our trials and used custom peak detection software to extract heart rate data. We predicted that the presence of cowbird eggs would cause stress in incubating female robins, with a corresponding increase in their heart rates. When comparing the audio from the cyan and beige egg trials of five subjects, there was no significant change in rate or variance. We conclude that the presence of beige eggs, even when warranting a rejection, did not result in a significant change in the heart rate of robins.

    Introduction:¶

    Brood parasitism is a phenomenon in which individuals of brood-parasitic species lay their eggs in another species’ nests. If the host individuals are unable to recognize the parasitic egg as foreign, they will raise and feed the hatchling like one of her own. However, not all species care for brood-parasitic young: due to the varying colors and patterns of bird eggs amongst different species, host individuals can distinguish between their own eggs and those of a brood parasite (Hanley 2017). American robins (Turdus migratorius) are songbirds that are parasitized by the obligately brood-parasitic brown-headed cowbird (Molothrus ater). Robin eggs are an immaculate, cyan color, while cowbird eggs are brown, spotty, and considerably smaller. Because of this, robins are adept at rejecting cowbird eggs (Rothstein 1982, Luro et al. 2018), which they do by puncturing the eggshell and ejecting the egg from the nest. Parasitic eggs provide several problems for mother birds; not only do mother birds have to provide excess food to care for an additional chick, but there may be competition as well between her own chicks and a non-kin (Hauber 2003). Brood parasites also have been documented to attack nests of hosts that reject parasitic eggs, which can force hosts into compliance (Hoover et al. 2007). These reasons can provide an explanation for a mother robin feeling fearful or stressed.

    Fear is a natural psychological response to danger or a threat and can have far-reaching consequences to ecosystems (Adolphs et al. 2013). There are many observed outcomes in response to a threat, which makes it a complex topic to unravel. An animal may respond to an immediate fear with an increased heart rate, that supplies a greater flow of blood to the brain and muscles to prepare them to a response (Steimer 2002), or by a reduced heart rate, with the intent of slowing bodily motions to the point of feigning death, usually exhibited in young animals when escape from a threat is impossible (Alboni et al. 2014). These phenomena are known as tachycardia and bradycardia, respectively (Causby et al. 1981). However, the pathways controlling innate fear and fear memory are different than those controlling these acute fear responses and much less is known about them, particularly in the wild. What is known is that heart rate is subject to fluctuation by several factors, including the season (Turbill et al. 2011). This experiment sought to analyze if robins are stressed by cowbirds and their brood, or if these eggs are a mild nuisance at best, and this will come from monitoring the heart rate of host mothers. Using R (R Core Team 2017), we developed methods of analyzing the heart rate data of robins which we gathered during our experiment. If the presence of cowbird eggs creates stress for mother robins, we should be able to observe an increase in the heart rate of robins during our beige egg trials.

    Methods:¶

    Heart Rate Recording Devices¶

    Like many other birds, while the mother robin incubates her eggs, she presses a soft featherless patch of skin up against them called her brood patch. The brood patch has an array of blood vessels across it, and the direct contact with the eggs allows the mother to warm her eggs with ease.

    The brood patch, due to its lack of feathers, also makes for a great method of monitoring the heart rate of nesting birds. Using unidirectional electret microphones imbued into imposter eggs, we can listen to the mother’s heart rate. We 3D printed mimetic eggs using a MakerBot Replicator Mini+ 3D Printer. The mimetic egg models are composed of two interlocking halves, each with a hole in the middle. These holes are roughly the size of the electret microphones we are using, which allows us to slip them in and out of the egg while assembling the models. We ordered additional mimetic eggs from Shapeways.com which are smaller inside and do not come apart. We used standard 3.5mm audio cables and cut off the ends opposite of the jack, exposing the inner wires. There are two wires inside these cables, a ground wire and a signal wire. There are two layers of rubber casing that surround these wires, the exterior black casing is first clipped to expose the wires. We then spliced off about an inch of the black casing and nothing else to expose the second casing. This casing was then removed the same as the black to expose the raw wires. Before soldering begins, this wire is threaded through the holes in the sides of the model egg halves such that the halves can now interlock together.

    We used both unidirectional and omnidirectional microphones. Unidirectional microphones can isolate the mother’s heartbeat, without picking up any unwanted outside noises as she’s incubating. However, the omnidirectional microphones have a wider range in case the mother is not sitting directly on top of her eggs. These microphones have one side which picks up audio, this faces outward to detect audio from objects that press against the eggshell. The other side of the microphones contain two metal terminals, a positive and a negative terminal (any microphone should have one of these terminals marked).

    Using a soldering kit, we soldered each exposed wire to its respective node on the microphone. The signal wire connects to the positive terminal, and the ground wire connects to the negative terminal. If metal from either the wires or solder from either node touches the other, the microphone will fail to pick up any sound and won’t work properly. Be sure to test the microphone by plugging it into a recording device. If no audio is being recorded, it is still possible to reapply the soldering. After the soldering has been done correctly, we wrapped one of the wires in electrical tape to avoid it possible touching the opposing wire. Then, the wire is pulled through until the microphone remains tightly placed facing outside one of the holes of the eggshell. Finally, we use half a balloon and cover it over the half of the egg that contains the microphone. It’s pulled down to the opposite, twisted tightly, and taped to the wire. This creates a smooth casing over the egg and protects the microphone without interfering with its recording capabilities. This balloon is painted a cyan color that is meant to match the color of American robin eggs. The mimetic eggs ordered from Shapeways.com are painted either the same cyan color as the microphone eggs, or a beige color. The beige color is designed to mimic cowbird eggs (Canniff et al. 2018). We exchange one of her eggs for this microphone-imbued model egg and plug it into a recorder. The recorders we used are Olympus models VN-8100PC and VN-722PC. The recorder is taped somewhere discretely on the tree so as to not arouse suspicion from the mother. The recorder is usually placed inside a plastic bag as well to protect it from environmental detriments such as rain. This has no effect on recording and is optional. This non-invasive technique has been used in the past (Arnold et al. 2011, Borneman et al. 2014) to monitor avian heart rates without causing distress.

    Field Procedure:¶

    This experiment took place at 1801-1899 County Road 1700 N, Urbana, IL 61802, from 6:00 to 12:00 CST through June and July of 2018. Our research was approved under IACUC protocol (17259). We tracked robin nests in the area (how many for your study?), marking the coordinates to revisit and keep track of the nest contents. We used extendable mirrors to check inside nests without handling them. During the monitoring of nests, we recorded date, time, temperature, and clutch size to account for any confounding variables. While a mother was either absent or flushed from the nest, one of her eggs was swapped out for a microphone-embedded mimic egg designed to blend in with the rest of her clutch. The egg we substituted was kept in a holding container where it could stay incubated for the duration of the experiment. The wire attached to the mimic egg would be carefully hidden by drilling or poking a small hole in the bottom of the nest and threading the wire through, connecting it to a recorder taped to the base of the tree. For several subjects, a tiny camouflaged camera was attached to a branch above the nest, allowing us to see and keep track of the eggs during the experiment without appearing invasive. The camera was not active during this part of the experiment. The recorder stayed on for several hours to gauge the mother’s average resting heart rate. After two hours, we collected the recorder while leaving the microphone egg and camera intact. We revisited the nest the next day and plug the recorder back in. We then quickly inserted either a beige or cyan-colored egg into the nest. The color was chosen randomly through random number generation, with an even number for cyan and an odd number for beige. Following two hours of monitoring, we returned to retrieve the cyan/beige egg, and then we will insert an egg of the opposite color. This is to control for how robins may respond to any egg being placed into their nest; it’s important to observe how they respond to cowbird eggs specifically and not just any egg. During the two-hour period in which the beige egg sits in the mother’s nest, the camera planted the day before is turned on to pinpoint what time she rejects the beige egg if at all. After we’ve gathered six hours of heartbeat data, two resting the day before and an additional two for each egg color, we collected all our materials, returned the robin egg, and moved to the next nest.
